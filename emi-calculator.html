The maths, simply

How your EMI is calculated

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Instalment — the fixed amount you pay every month until the loan is closed. It contains both interest and principal, in proportions that shift over time.

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ [(1 + r)n − 1]

P = principal · r = monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100) · n = number of monthly instalments

In the early years, most of your EMI goes towards interest. As the balance falls, a larger share goes towards principal. That is why prepaying early saves more than prepaying late.

Four ways to reduce your EMI

  • Improve your credit score. A 750+ CIBIL score can unlock a meaningfully lower rate.
  • Add a co-applicant. A second income can raise eligibility and improve pricing.
  • Choose a longer tenure carefully. It lowers the EMI but raises total interest — use our schedule to see the trade-off.
  • Make part-prepayments. Even one extra EMI a year can cut years off a long home loan.
Rule of thumb: keep all your EMIs together below 40–50% of your net monthly income. If a proposed EMI pushes you past that, a smaller loan is usually the wiser choice.
Calculator questions

EMI FAQs

Is this EMI calculator accurate?

It uses the standard reducing-balance formula that banks and NBFCs use for most retail loans. Your actual EMI may differ slightly due to the lender's rounding, processing fee capitalisation, insurance bundling or a different day-count convention.

Does the EMI change during the loan?

On a fixed-rate loan, the EMI stays constant. On a floating-rate loan, the EMI or the tenure can change when the benchmark rate moves. Your lender must communicate any reset in advance.

Should I choose a longer tenure to lower the EMI?

A longer tenure improves monthly affordability but increases total interest. Choose the shortest tenure whose EMI you can comfortably service in a bad month — not the longest one you can technically qualify for.

What is a good EMI-to-income ratio?

Most lenders prefer total EMIs below 40–50% of net monthly income. Staying under 40% leaves room for emergencies and protects your credit profile.
